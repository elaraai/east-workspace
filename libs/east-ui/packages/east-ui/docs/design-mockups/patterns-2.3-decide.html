The decision-maker's job

The user of a Decide-mode surface is a frontline business decision-maker — a demand planner, store ops lead, buyer, scheduler, category manager, pricing analyst. They have 5–15 minutes per decision in a queue of dozens-to-hundreds per week. They carry private information the model cannot have: a conversation with a customer yesterday, a quality concern overheard on the floor, a regulatory whisper, weather they saw out the window. They are accountable for outcomes — they get evaluated, and they know it. Years of domain judgement sit beside the model's math.

Their job is not to verify that the model's optimisation is optimal. Their job is to commit a defensible decision quickly that combines what the model knows with what they know, and that they can stand behind when their boss, a peer, or an auditor asks why. The platform's job, said in their language, is: "Give me the evidence I need to trust, modify, or override this rec — fast — and let me show my working when someone asks."

What "evidence for judgement" means

Decide-mode patterns serve seven kinds of evidence. Each kind earns its place by changing what the decision-maker does:

  • The recommendation as an argument — claim, supporting reasons, upside, risks, unknowns, ask. The model's case stated the way an executive briefing states it, not as a card with three slots of free text.
  • What the model doesn't know — epistemic gaps (no comparable cases, novel scenario, model has been wrong here), not just aleatoric uncertainty.
  • Stakes in human terms — "$80k impact, affects 3 people, reversible 24h" — not button colour.
  • Reference class — "last 12 like this you accepted 9, were right 7"; "3 of 5 peers chose X"; "this kind of rec works ~73% of the time".
  • Risks named in plain language, not buried in confidence intervals.
  • A clean way to commit, modify, or reject with reason captured — defensible later, ideally in the user's own voice.
  • Their own track record — surfaced from §2.6 Calibrate, referenced inline here when relevant.

Decision archetypes served

The same Decide surface serves several different jobs depending on the kind of decision. The patterns in this spec are tagged for each archetype below; not every pattern serves every archetype.

Cross-cutting commitments

  • Patches not callbacks. Every commit affordance emits a Patch<TState> rather than calling an opaque handler. Preview (East.applyPatch), composition (East.composePatch), and undo (East.invertPatch) all share one shape.
  • Reason capture is mandatory on Modify and Override. Accept may be silent; modifying or overriding the model's rec without reason is a documentation gap that hurts the user at review time.
  • Status colour always paired with an icon (§0.3). WCAG-friendly; never rely on hue alone.
  • Numerals in JetBrains Mono with tabular-nums. Stakes, deltas, ranges align across the surface.
  • Stakes tag is mandatory on every commit affordance. The user must always know what they're committing to in human terms.
  • Reference patterns are inset, not modal. They live alongside the briefing, not in dialogs that interrupt flow.

2.3.1 Recommendation.Briefing

anchor Recommendation.*

"What should I do, and on what evidence?"

Purpose

The Recommendation.Briefing is the canonical Decide-mode pattern: it presents the model's recommendation as a structured argument. Six named slots — claim, because, upside, risks, unknowns, ask — force the recommendation into a shape the user can read in 30 seconds and defend in a meeting.

Where a generic action card invites a paragraph of free text, the Briefing requires the rec to commit to a claim, support it with at most three because bullets, name its upside in the user's currency, name its risks in plain language, surface what the model doesn't know (so the user knows where to apply private knowledge), and end with an ask — three commit affordances of escalating reasoning weight: Apply, Modify, Override.

The Briefing is the spine of every Decide-mode screen. Reference, Judgement, and Stakes patterns flank it; commit affordances live in its ask row.

Slots
Slot Type Required Description
claim UIComp · 1 line required The recommendation as a single concrete imperative sentence. Subject + verb + object + qualifier. Not a question, not a description.
because Array<UIComp> · max 3 required Up to three supporting reasons, ordered by importance. Each reason is a single sentence; an inline accent (parenthetical) carries the supporting number. More than 3 reasons hides the strongest; truncate ruthlessly.
upside UIComp required The benefit of acting, in the user's primary currency (revenue, margin, hours, units). Lead with the headline number; supporting metrics follow inline.
risks UIComp required if material Named risks in plain language. If empty, render the row as "none material" rather than omitting (decision-makers notice silent omissions). Avoid burying risks in confidence intervals.
unknowns UIComp required if any What the model didn't have at decision time. Typically what the user is supposed to bring. Pairs with Judgement.Gap.
stakes Stakes.Tag required Inline chip with impact + affected count + reversibility. Always present; mandatory across the family.
ask { apply, modify?, override?, secondary?: UIComp } required Three commit affordances. apply = primary; modify opens ModifyAndCommit; override opens OverrideWithReason. Secondary slot is for non-commit links (e.g. "why not alternatives?").
accent "brand" | "warn" | "danger" default brand Left-rail tint. warn for high-stakes guarded recs; danger for irreversible. Auto-derived from commitStrength if not set.
Behaviour
  • Apply emits the rec's Patch<TState> through the standard commit pipeline. commitStrength grades friction: trivial applies inline; standard opens Commit.Confirm; guarded requires audit note; irreversible requires typed confirmation + audit note.
  • Modify opens ModifyAndCommit with the rec's patch as the editable starting point. The user adjusts; the resulting patch goes through commit with a "modified" tag in the audit and a structured reason captured.
  • Override + why opens OverrideWithReason with no committed patch — the user commits a no-op (or their own patch) with a structured reason. Always reason-required; the model's rec is preserved in audit alongside the override.
  • The "Why this and not alternatives?" link opens AlternativesList in a side drawer. Optional — render only when alternatives exist with material differences.
  • Reference patterns (Similar, Peers, Base) are typically rendered below or beside the briefing rather than inside it; the briefing stays compact. Some apps will inline a single Reference chip in the eyebrow when space is tight.
  • Capture-to-audit: every commit captures the briefing's claim, the chosen action (apply/modify/override), the patch, and any reason text. The Briefing is the source of the audit record's headline.
  • Keyboard: ⏎ applies; M modifies; O overrides. Decide-mode screens never auto-focus the Apply button without a non-trivial commitStrength guard.
States
default
Brand left-rail. Apply is primary, Modify and Override are secondary/ghost. Used for routine and standard archetypes.
guarded (warn)
Orange left-rail. Apply opens Commit.Confirm with required audit note. Used for Commitment and material Strategic decisions.
irreversible (danger)
Red left-rail; Apply opens Commit.Confirm with typed confirmation + audit note. Override remains a path; Modify hidden.
applied
After commit, the briefing collapses into a 1-line summary with timestamp + author, plus an Undo affordance while the rec is reversible. Adjacent AuditTrail entry surfaces the full record.
stale
If the inputs the rec was based on have changed since computation, the briefing renders with a FreshnessChip (state: dirty) and a Refresh action that re-runs and re-renders. Apply is disabled while stale.
superseded
When a newer rec for the same target has arrived, the briefing renders with a "newer rec available" banner and link. The current briefing remains until the user explicitly switches.
computing
When the rec is being recomputed (e.g. after a Judgement.Inject), the briefing renders with a skeleton claim line + spinner; commit affordances disabled.
no-rec
When the model has no recommendation (e.g. infeasible inputs), the briefing renders with the claim "No action recommended" + a prominent Reference.Novelty or ComputeError explaining why.
When to use

Recommendation.Briefing is the default for any single-rec Decide screen across all archetypes. The exceptions:

Routine Exception Commitment Strategic Reactive People

For Routine in bulk (e.g. 50 daily reorder recs), the Briefing renders inside Decision.Queue rows in a collapsed form (claim + stakes only); the user expands one at a time when they need to dig in. For People archetype, Override + why is mandatory regardless of commitStrength — the platform refuses to commit a People decision without a structured reason.

Rationale & non-obvious choices
  • Why three reasons max? Decision research (Miller's law, Hick's law) and our own queue-time data both say three reasons read; five hide the strongest. The cap is a forcing function on the model — it must commit to its top three, not list ten.
  • Why "don't know" as a first-class slot? Most decision-quality literature treats epistemic uncertainty as a footnote. For our user the gap is their value-add — it's where their private knowledge is supposed to enter. Naming it tells them where to look.
  • Why three commit affordances, not one or two? Apply-only treats the user as a rubber-stamp; Apply/Reject treats overrides as failures. Apply / Modify / Override + why treats all three as legitimate, and modify is where most of the value-added action happens (the user knows something, adjusts, commits).
  • Why upside before risks? The model is biased toward proposing — its job is to surface action. The Briefing leads with the upside the model thinks justifies the action, so the user can audit the model's claim before reading risks. Inverting (risks first) primes the user to override defensively.
  • Why "Override + why" in the button label? Discourages drive-by rejection. The button says what the action requires; clicking it commits to writing.
